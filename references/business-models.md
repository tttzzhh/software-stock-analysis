# Software Business Models

Classify the revenue engine before interpreting growth or valuation. Many companies are hybrids; analyze each material stream separately and then explain mix shifts.

## Seat-based subscriptions

Revenue driver:

`paid seats × realized price per seat × recognized contract fraction`

Leading indicators include customer count, seats per customer, enterprise mix, renewal rate, NRR, price increases, and seat activation. The model offers visibility when contracts are durable, but it is exposed to hiring cycles, shelfware, seat consolidation, and enterprise-wide bundles.

Questions:

- Is the buyer paying for provisioned, contracted, or active users?
- Can customers reduce seats before renewal?
- Does AI reduce required seats or justify a premium tier?
- Are price increases tied to new functionality or simply renewal leverage?

## Usage- or consumption-based

Revenue driver:

`customers × activity per customer × realized unit price`

Leading indicators include workloads, data volume, compute, API calls, active developers, committed spend, consumption relative to commitments, and cloud optimization. This model can expand naturally with customer activity but has less near-term visibility and is sensitive to optimization, unit-price declines, and workload migration.

Separate underlying usage growth from realized revenue growth. Falling unit costs may improve customer value while slowing reported revenue unless elasticity offsets pricing declines.

## Transaction- or volume-based

Revenue driver:

`gross volume × take rate + subscriptions and other fees`

Track gross payment volume, ad spend, marketplace value, trades, bookings, or another sector-specific volume measure. Analyze customer concentration, mix by geography or payment method, pass-through revenue, incentive costs, fraud or credit losses, and take-rate compression. Gross volume is not revenue.

## Outcome-based pricing

Revenue is tied to a verified result such as revenue generated, cost saved, issue resolved, fraud prevented, or successful workflow completion. Assess:

- whether the outcome is observable and attributable;
- the baseline used to measure incremental value;
- audit and dispute mechanisms;
- timing between delivery, verification, billing, and revenue recognition;
- customer willingness to share upside;
- tail risk when outcomes depend on factors outside the vendor's control.

Outcome pricing can align value and price, but it may create volatile revenue, implementation burden, disputes, and balance-sheet exposure.

## Hybrid models

Common combinations include platform fee plus consumption, base seats plus AI usage, subscription plus transaction take rate, and committed spend with overage. Decompose growth into:

1. customer additions;
2. expansion or contraction;
3. price and packaging;
4. usage or transaction volume;
5. mix shifts;
6. acquisitions and currency.

Identify which component leads recognized revenue and which component makes it volatile.

## Economic comparison

| Model | Visibility | Natural expansion | Main downside | Useful metrics |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Seat | Usually high | Seats and modules | Hiring slowdown, shelfware, bundling | Seats, NRR, logo retention, RPO |
| Usage | Medium | Workload growth | Optimization, unit-price decline | Consumption, commitments, usage per customer |
| Transaction | Cyclical | Volume and take rate | Macro volume, mix, regulation | Gross volume, take rate, loss rate |
| Outcome | Variable | Share of measurable value | Attribution and revenue volatility | Outcomes, realized fee, verification cycle |

## Output block

For each company, state:

- customer and economic buyer;
- pricing unit and contract mechanics;
- revenue equation and leading indicators;
- renewal and expansion engine;
- gross-margin and infrastructure implications;
- sensitivity to hiring, IT budgets, rates, transactions, cloud optimization, and AI;
- whether reported KPIs actually observe the claimed revenue driver.
